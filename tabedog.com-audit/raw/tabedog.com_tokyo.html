Best restaurants in Tokyo

Tokyo has more restaurants than any city on earth, around 150,000, and more Michelin stars than Paris, but the real guide locals use is Tabelog. The scores are what separate the 3.1 sushi counter by your hotel from the 3.7 one two blocks away that you would never find otherwise. Tokyo is also where Tabelog's grading is harshest: the sheer competition means a 3.5 here is a top-3% restaurant, and the city's 4.0-rated places are among the best meals in the world.

Eating well in Tokyo is less about picking the right restaurant than picking the right neighborhood and then trusting the numbers. Ginza for sushi and tempura, Shinjuku and Ikebukuro for ramen, Asakusa for old Edo cooking, Ebisu and Nakameguro for izakaya and bistros, Tsukiji for seafood breakfast. TabeDog puts all of it on one English map with hours and prices so you can decide in thirty seconds instead of an hour of translating.

Where to eat in Tokyo, by neighborhood

  • Ginza the highest density of top-rated sushi and tempura counters in Japan, plus lunch omakase at a fraction of dinner prices
  • Shinjuku Omoide Yokocho yakitori alleys, Golden Gai bars and a wall of 3.5+ ramen shops around the station
  • Shibuya casual izakaya, ramen and the Shibuya Yokocho food alleys; busy late
  • Asakusa century-old tempura, unagi, soba and sukiyaki houses near Senso-ji
  • Tsukiji the outer market: seafood breakfast, tamagoyaki and sushi from 6 a.m.
  • Ebisu & Nakameguro the best neighborhood izakaya, bistros and natural-wine bars in the city
  • Ueno & Akihabara Ameyoko market stalls, cheap kaisendon and old-school tonkatsu
  • Ikebukuro a ramen battleground with several Top-100 shops within ten minutes of the station
  • Roppongi high-end yakiniku, teppanyaki and the most English-friendly dining in Tokyo
  • Shimokitazawa the cult curry capital of Tokyo, plus vintage kissaten

What to eat in Tokyo

  • Edomae sushi aged and cured nigiri the way Tokyo invented it
  • Tokyo shoyu ramen clear soy-based broth, the classic style, perfected in Ogikubo and Ikebukuro
  • Tempura counter tempura is a Tokyo (Edo) creation
  • Tonkatsu born in Ueno; the city's best shops still cluster there
  • Monjayaki Tsukishima's runnier cousin of okonomiyaki
  • Unagi steamed-then-grilled eel in lacquered boxes at century-old houses

Reading Tabelog scores in Tokyo

Tokyo scores skew low because reviewers here have eaten everywhere. Treat 3.3 as good, 3.5 as excellent, and anything above 3.7 as worth crossing the city for. Tabelog's Hyakumeiten (Top 100) lists for Tokyo ramen, sushi, tonkatsu and yakitori are the most reliable shortlists in the city.

Tokyo restaurant FAQ

What food is Tokyo famous for?

Edomae sushi, Tokyo shoyu ramen, Tempura, Tonkatsu, Monjayaki, Unagi. Aged and cured nigiri the way Tokyo invented it.

Where should I eat in Tokyo?

Ginza for the highest density of top-rated sushi and tempura counters in Japan, plus lunch omakase at a fraction of dinner prices; Shinjuku for Omoide Yokocho yakitori alleys, Golden Gai bars and a wall of 3.5+ ramen shops around the station; Shibuya for casual izakaya, ramen and the Shibuya Yokocho food alleys; busy late. What is a good Tabelog rating in Tokyo?

Is Tabelog available in English for Tokyo?

Tabelog has a limited English site, but most listings, reviews and the useful details (hours, prices, payment methods) are only in Japanese. TabeDog shows Tokyo's restaurants on an English map with those details translated, using Tabelog's own ratings.

How do I avoid tourist traps in Tokyo?

Use the Tabelog score, not the sign. Restaurants with big English signs and touts near Ginza typically rate 3.0 or lower; the ones locals queue for rate 3.4 and up and are often a street or two away. TabeDog puts both on the same map so the difference is visible.
