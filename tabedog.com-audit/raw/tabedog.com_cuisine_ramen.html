Ramen in Japan

Tonkotsu, shoyu, miso and tsukemen, ranked by the people who eat it daily.

Ramen is the one cuisine where Tabelog's ratings matter most. Japan has over 30,000 ramen shops and the difference between a 3.2 and a 3.7 is the difference between a forgettable bowl and a line around the block. Every region has its own style: pork-bone tonkotsu in Fukuoka, miso in Sapporo, clear shoyu in Tokyo, thick tsukemen dipping noodles everywhere. Most shops are tiny counters that seat ten people, cash only, with a ticket machine by the door.

Best cities for ramen

Best ramen in Tokyo

Tokyo's ramen scene is the deepest in Japan: the Hyakumeiten Top 100 for Tokyo ramen alone is longer than most cities' entire lists. Shoyu is the native style, but every regional style has a flagship shop here.

Shinjuku · Ikebukuro · Shibuya · Ogikubo · Kanda

Best ramen in Osaka

Osaka ramen is underrated: a strong tonkotsu-shoyu tradition around Namba, Kyobashi and Fukushima, plus late-night bowls on Dotonbori until dawn. Several Tabelog Hyakumeiten shops are within walking distance of Namba station..

Namba · Fukushima · Kyobashi · Umeda

Best ramen in Kyoto

Ichijoji, a student district in the north of the city, has more highly rated ramen shops per block than anywhere in Japan, and Kyoto's own style, a thick dark chicken-pork soy broth, was born here. Kyoto Station has a ramen street inside the building for quick bowls before the train..

Ichijoji · Kyoto Station · Kawaramachi

Best ramen in Fukuoka

This is where tonkotsu was invented, and Fukuoka's ramen is thinner-noodled, faster and more intense than the tonkotsu exported to the rest of the world. The original shops around Hakata station and Nagahama fish market are open past midnight, kaedama (a second serving of noodles) costs ¥100, and the Nakasu yatai serve bowls at riverside stalls.

Hakata · Nagahama · Nakasu · Tenjin

Best ramen in Sapporo

Miso ramen was invented in Sapporo and the city's bowls are heavier and more buttery than anywhere else, often with corn and a knob of butter on top, built for minus-ten winters. Ramen Yokocho in Susukino is the famous alley but Tabelog's top-rated miso shops are scattered around Susukino and Sapporo Station, and several run late into the night..

Susukino · Sapporo Station · Odori

Best ramen in Nagoya

Nagoya's ramen is Taiwan ramen: a spicy minced-pork and chive bowl invented at a Taiwanese restaurant in Imaike in the 1970s. The original is still open.

Imaike · Nagoya Station · Sakae

Best ramen in Yokohama

Iekei ramen was invented in Yokohama in 1974: a rich pork-and-chicken soy broth with thick noodles, spinach and nori, customized by richness, noodle firmness and oil. The original shop and its direct lineage are rated far above the chains, and Tabelog makes the lineage visible.

Shin-Yokohama · Yokohama Station · Kannai

Best ramen in Hiroshima

Hiroshima has two noodle traditions: a mild soy ramen and the city's own tsukemen, cold noodles dipped into a chili-hot sesame sauce at a spice level you choose. The famous tsukemen shops are around Hatchobori and Nagarekawa and many run late for the bar crowd..

Hatchobori · Nagarekawa · Hiroshima Station

How to order ramen

Buy a ticket from the vending machine before you sit down (most have photos, some now have English). Hand the ticket to the staff and say nothing else unless asked about noodle firmness (katame is firm, futsu is normal). Eat fast, slurp, and leave; lingering at a ramen counter is rude when people are waiting outside.

Ticket machines increasingly have English, and the choice is usually just soup type, noodle size and toppings, so ordering without Japanese is easy.
